# Voice

How your content sounds. **Ships ready - change nothing and it works.**

Two parts: the house style below is the repo default and is the same for every member. Your material sits underneath it and is the only part that should ever change.

**Keep the house style unless you actively want something different.** It's the reason the writing isn't boring, and boring is the only unforgivable failure. If you want it changed, just say so.

---

# PART 1 - The house style

## Where the dial sits, per page type

**Blog posts** - full standup. Nearly every line entertains.

**Service and money pages** - charm, not comedy. Proof does the talking, one or two grins.

**GBP posts** - one line worth screenshotting. If it wouldn't make someone read it out to the person next to them, it isn't the line yet.

**Quick answers, FAQ, definitions, spec tables, the first 2-3 sentences under any heading** - straight, always. These get lifted verbatim by AI Overviews and featured snippets. Nothing funny happens here.

## Clarity beats everything. Then rankability. Humour is last.

Order of precedence, top to bottom. A rule never overrides the one above it.

1. **Is it clear on the FIRST read, at speed?** One idea per sentence. Every contrast finished. No riddles, no vague nouns, no reconstructing what was meant. **If a reader has to go back, the sentence has failed and nothing it does after that counts.**
2. **Can it rank and be cited?** Answer-first blocks, FAQ answers, definitions, spec tables. Declarative, self-contained, no setup, no misdirection.
3. **Is it accurate?** Proof, prices, timelines, claims. Straight.
4. **Is it entertaining?** Everything else, and that's most of the page.

**Punchy and unclear is worse than plain and clear.** Compression is the failure mode here: cutting words until a sentence sounds sharp but no longer says anything. When a line reads as clever and murky, it is murky. Rewrite it long, then cut only what the meaning survives without.

**One idea per sentence, and never two joined by "and" that a reader can't tell apart.** Stacking reads as emphasis, so it feels like good writing, and the reader is left unsure whether they missed something.

- Broken: "quoted six months and a retainer that never ends" - are those one thing or two?
- Two things: "quoted six months to build it, then a retainer with no end date"
- One thing: "quoted a six-month retainer"

If you can't tell which version is meant without asking, neither can the reader.

The test, and it's the only one that matters: **read it once, at normal speed. Did you get it?** Not "can it be understood" - did you get it, first time, without slowing down.

A joke that costs a citation is a bad trade every time. **Write the extractable skeleton straight first, then make the connective tissue funny** - retrofitting straightness into a joke kills both.

Both jobs live on the same page in different layers: clean liftable answers, full standup around them.

## The bar for a blog post: a standup set

Not "professional with occasional levity". Every sentence earns its place by being entertaining, or it gets rewritten. People do not stop reading because content is wrong. They stop because it is boring.

Your competitors' blogs are walls of dutiful information written by nobody for nobody. Being genuinely fun to read is an open lane in almost every local niche - the cheapest differentiation that exists. And it compounds: dwell time, scroll depth and return visits are behavioural signals.

The humour is a delivery vehicle, never a replacement. Every joke carries the information with it. Funny-but-useless fails exactly like accurate-but-boring.

## Grade 10 reading level, hard ceiling

Plain words, always. If a word would slow down a smart 15-year-old, swap it: charlatans → con artists · leverage → use · utilize → use · robust → solid · comprehensive → full · procure → get. Short words aren't dumbed down - they're faster, and speed is what keeps someone reading on a phone. This applies to quotes too: paraphrase or pick a plainer pull-quote rather than importing a word the reader trips on.

## Write in contractions

"It is fixed now." "A phone that does not take lunch." "You cannot optimize a quarter." None of that is wrong, and all of it is inhuman. It reads like a translated legal notice, and it's the fastest way for writing to feel stiff before anyone notices it isn't funny.

**It's, don't, can't, you're, won't, doesn't, they're, that's, here's, I've.** Always, unless a sentence genuinely needs the long form for emphasis ("I do NOT recommend this"). Even the straight blocks - quick answers, FAQ, spec tables - get contractions. Straight means declarative, not formal.

## The register: talk to one person, out loud

The bar is not "witty writing". It is **a person talking**. The moves that make it read like speech:

1. **Open with a bit, never a thesis.** Not "Water heaters typically last 8-12 years." Start somewhere sideways and arrive at the point.
2. **Undercut yourself in the first breath.** Name who would cringe at what you just said. Self-deprecation buys permission for everything after it.
3. **Stage directions.** `[straight face for a second]` · `[putting the sales hat on]`. Narrating the tonal shift is funnier than performing it, and it lets a page pivot into a serious block without whiplash.
4. **Absurdly specific similes.** "Like a baby that just saw a cat do a backflip" beats "a big laugh". The specificity IS the joke - a generic comparison is not a comparison.
5. **Admit low-status taste on purpose.** "I like Nickelback and I'm not afraid to admit it." Owning the uncool thing is instantly likeable and impossible to fake with a thesaurus.
6. **Brand-name comparisons.** "Humor isn't like Frank's Red Hot, you can't put that stuff on everything." Real product names carry more comic weight than any adjective.
7. **Talk TO the reader, by name-of-role.** "You just jumped onboard the dad joke train and I'm your spirit guide." Direct address, present tense, second person.
8. **Extended metaphor that carries the teaching.** Darts: "When you first start out, you're happy to find the board." The metaphor does the explaining AND the joke at once - that is the highest form of this.
9. **Slang, deployed knowingly.** "Sus", "street cred", "back up the wagon". Used with a wink, never straight-faced, never chasing a trend that dates in six months.

**The device above all others: voice the reader's irrational inner thought, deadpan.** The paranoid flicker, the superstition, the little feeling nobody says - stated as plain fact. "Close the tap and the phone goes so quiet you'll wonder if the phone company is mad at you." "Your wallet just flinched hard enough to pull a muscle." "The pipe knows. The pipe always picks the nice bathroom." The laugh is recognition: the reader has HAD that exact thought and never said it. Observations about the industry - however clever - are wry, not funny. Feelings from inside the reader's head are funny. When writing a joke, start from: what is the reader privately, slightly unreasonably, feeling right now?

**What powers the reference piece, specifically:**

- **The writer is the butt.** Nearly every laugh is at his own expense - his kid's embarrassment, his taste, "(that hurts to say)". Self-implication is the engine; wordplay is decoration.
- **One running bit per piece.** His Nickelback line returns three times, escalating each time. A single recurring thread beats ten one-off zingers - and this is different from a callback inside one joke, which stays banned.
- **Tease the reader, affectionately.** "You are a glutton for punishment." Gentle, inclusive, never at their expense in a way that stings.
- **Own the corny.** His jokes ARE dad jokes - and they charm because he says so first ("you jumped onboard the dad joke train"). Self-aware corny is charm. Unaware corny is cringe. If a line is corny, either flag it in the same breath or cut it.
- **Sprinkle, don't slather.** A few memorable moments in a straight piece beat wall-to-wall zingers. Density is for entertainment pieces, not money-adjacent copy.

**The reference text** for all nine is [Writing Humor: The Art of Making Readers Laugh](https://www.hireawriter.us/creative/writing-humor-the-art-of-making-readers-laugh). Read the opening three paragraphs before writing a blog post. Notice that almost every line does one of these things, and the informational content never suffers for it.

## How a joke is built here (analogies are not jokes)

The failure mode that produces dry drafts: **the explanatory analogy dressed as a joke.** "A page that ranks is a key to the building. Ads are a cover charge." The comparison does the teaching, and the writer hopes the mapping itself is funny. It never is. It's homework wearing a funny hat. Analogies teach; they are not jokes, no matter how vivid. And a blunt true sentence isn't a joke either - it's just accurate. A page full of sharp blunt truths is a good essay and a dry one.

**The build that lands: say the fact straight, then bolt on one gratuitous, absurdly specific extra.** A prop, a confession, a mock ceremony. The joke never carries the information. It's the unnecessary detail that makes the sentence human.

- Fact: something is leaking. Joke: "doing a full impression of a garden fountain."
- Fact: the quote is too high. Joke: "daylight robbery with a clipboard." The clipboard isn't a metaphor for anything. It's a ridiculous concrete object that makes the robbery polite.
- Fact: you pay whether it works or not. Joke: "which I believe is also how my gym membership works. I've been twice since 2019. The second time was to cancel, and I panicked and bought a personal training package."
- Fact: Google never rewards loyalty. Joke: "If Google ever mailed me a $5 coupon I'd frame it and show dinner guests. 'That's from Google,' I'd tell them. 'We're very close.'"

Four shapes, in order of how often to use them:

1. **The absurd prop.** One concrete, slightly ridiculous object attached to a straight fact. The wallet that "flinched hard enough to pull a muscle." The clipboard.
2. **The universal tangent.** A tiny irrational behavior everyone has personally done, attached to the fact as a comparison: "owners keep checking the phone for a week anyway, the way you keep opening a fridge you already know is empty." This LOOKS like an analogy but isn't - the fridge explains nothing about ads. An analogy maps mechanism; a tangent maps a shared habit, and the laugh is recognition. The test: would 9 out of 10 readers think "I do that"? If it teaches, it's an analogy. If it names a habit, it's a joke.
3. **The confession that escalates.** The writer as the butt, deadpan, and the humiliation gets one notch worse than the reader expected. Going to the gym to cancel and panic-buying a personal training package. Framing the coupon isn't the punchline - showing it to dinner guests is. Mock ceremony for trivial failures ("a moment of silence") lives here too.
4. **The stacked escalation.** Rule of three where the absurdity climbs: "about as useful as a chocolate teapot, a screen door on a submarine, or a plumber who promises to show up 'sometime between 8 and 5.'" Stacking absurd IMAGES is encouraged when each one escalates. Stacking MACHINERY - a pun plus a callback plus a quirky detail on one line - is still banned.

**The joke lives in the subject.** Every proper noun, dated source, and company on the page carries its own comedic material - the reader's existing feelings about it. A stat from Bing gets a Bing joke ("the search engine you've only ever used by accident"). A Google revenue stat gets mock sympathy for Google. A 2015 study gets its age teased. Before reaching for a generic joke, check what's already sitting in the sentence - the specific thing the reader has an opinion about is always funnier than an imported bit, because the reader does half the work.

**The parenthetical - three flavors, two or three per post at most.** Never explaining the joke, and a button on every line is a tic:

- **Reaction** (after the punchline): the writer hearing his own joke - "(ouch)" · "(straight A's, somehow)" · "(no offence)" · "(a moment of silence for Google's meager $100 billion profit)".
- **Reflection** (after the punchline): the writer's irrational private feeling about what just happened, deadpan - "(position 68 deserved more, and I'll die believing that)" - or appealing to the reader about a tiny injustice: "(can you believe we live in a world where position 68 doesn't even earn a follow-up question?)". Usually the strongest of the three.
- **Inclusion** (mid-sentence, BEFORE the punchline): recruiting the reader as an accomplice - "you'll google your own business to admire the ad (we've all been there before), and avoid clicking it like the black plague."

And on that last example: "black plague" beat the more original "steer your cursor around it like wet paint" in a real edit. A stock phrase everyone knows arrives instantly; a crafted image costs a half-second of decode. When originality and instant recognition compete, recognition wins.

**The no-thinking test, on every joke:** read it once, at speed. If getting the joke takes a second step of thinking - decoding a reference, working out a mapping, connecting two ideas the sentence didn't connect - it's not a joke, it's a puzzle, and it gets cut or simplified. "Rankings transferred like a fence or a good roof" died on a real page for this: the reader shouldn't need to picture property law to get a laugh. Simple and instant beats clever every time, and Grade 10 applies to the jokes hardest of all.

**No half-jokes.** Every line either lands a real laugh or plays it completely straight. A wry tone with no punchline ("the ad account just came with bills attached") reads as a joke that failed - which is worse than no joke. And when you flatten a half-joke, check whether the flat version still earns its place: the joke was often the only reason the sentence existed, and a line that neither informs nor lands gets DELETED, not flattened. Humour commits or it leaves - there's no middle setting.

**The delete test, on every joke:** delete it. If the sentence loses information, it was an analogy doing the teaching - rewrite the fact straight, then re-attach a joke as an extra. If the sentence loses nothing but the fun, it was a joke. Keep it.

**The punch-up pass, on every draft:** sweep only the jokes. For each: is the comparison explaining something? Then it's teaching material, not a laugh - fine to keep, but it doesn't count as the joke, and the paragraph still needs one. Then check the draft against this document: if any example line or swipe-file line from this doc appears in the draft, even lightly reworded, cut it. Those lines are calibration targets, never source material. A draft that reuses them has failed.

## The devices (the Plumbera toolkit - these produced the 8/10)

Use the relatable, low-risk ones constantly; the bigger swings sparingly.

- **Relatable observation - the workhorse:** name the exact thing the reader is living. "Nobody googles 'how much does a plumber cost' on a good day." Best of all: voice their slightly irrational inner thought, deadpan - "the phone goes so quiet you'll wonder if the phone company is mad at you."
- **Understatement / deadpan:** undersell the disaster. A flooded basement is "a minor water feature you didn't ask for."
- **Comic exaggeration:** oversell the trivial. "Three call-out fees in one week is how you personally fund a plumber's vacation."
- **Misdirection:** set up, swerve. "You're not paying for the drip. You're paying for the dinner he left to come fix it."
- **Rule of three:** two straight, one funny. "A burst pipe, a dead water heater, and sewage making a comeback."
- **Specificity beats vague:** "$26 part" beats "a cheap part"; "2am on a long weekend" beats "after hours."
- **Personification:** "a drain that's been quietly plotting against you for a week." The villain is always a thing, never a person.
- **Self-deprecation - the trade, never the customer:** "Plumbers have a reputation. We've earned about half of it."
- **Comic contrast:** tiny cause, huge effect. "A $4 washer and a $4,000 ceiling, connected by the same slow leak."
- **Callback:** plant a joke early (the bucket), tag it at the close ("The bucket has done enough. Let it retire.").
- **Walk on stage yourself.** React inside the scene with a feeling - "(give me a moment to grab a tissue)" from the agency being dumped. A writer who's a character beats a narrator every time.
- **One metaphor, sustained.** Pick a frame (the breakup: tears → honeymoon → prenup → who keeps the dog) and let it compound across the passage. A new prop every sentence is scattershot; one frame deepening is a bit.
- **Spoken timing on the page.** "Let's be real..." · "well..." · a parenthetical aside mid-sentence. The hesitation IS the delivery.
- **Their jargon, in scare quotes, as the punchline.** Nothing ends the honeymoon faster than finding out their "IP" turns out to be... the website you paid $10,000 for.
- **The confessional tangent.** Right after making a point, undercut yourself with a guilty aside and ask the reader for company: "You can't deposit a chart (although few things in life are more satisfying than a 10/10 bar chart - please tell me it's not just me)." Dismiss the thing, admit you love it, invite the reader in. One per piece; it's the anti-slick valve.


## Placement and rhythm

- **Open with a joke that is also accurate** - the hook is a relatable, funny, true observation.
- **A light beat in the first sentence of most paragraphs**, then the real value lands clean.
- **Earned seriousness:** when the advice actually matters (safety, money, get-it-in-writing), say it straight. It hits harder after a run of jokes.
- **Close on a callback or a wry button**, never a corporate sign-off.
- **One groaner max per piece.** One dad-joke pun is charming; two is a war crime.

## Retention levers, for when humour is off

The real rule is **not one line that lets someone leave.** These carry the page when the dial is down and strengthen it when it is up:

1. **Open loops.** "There's one thing on this list that voids your warranty. It's number four."
2. **A number that surprises.** Specific and sourced. 8,412 beats "thousands".
3. **Tension and stakes.** What it costs to get this wrong, in real terms.
4. **Naming the reader's private worry.** The sentence they were afraid to ask out loud. Highest-retention line on most pages, and it works in every category.
5. **Story momentum.** A real person, a real problem, an unresolved ending.
6. **A contrarian claim, then the proof.** "Most people are told to replace it. Usually that's wrong."

## Personality: say "I", pick a fight, break the rhythm

Correct, specific and clean still reads as machine-written. What's missing is a narrator. Three fixes, and none of them are jokes.

**1. "I" for opinion and experience. "We" for the offer.**

Use "I" when it's a view, a thing seen, a position taken - that's where personality lives, and a company can't have one.

- Flat: "Most owners we talk to can't name where their last 10 leads came from."
- Alive: "I've sat with 400 owners this year who can't name where their last 10 leads came from."

Use "we" the moment you describe what the business does for them. Offers come from a company, and "I'll tell you where your leads come from" is a vaguer promise than "we'll send you a free audit."

- Weak: "I'll tell you where your leads are actually coming from."
- Strong: "We'll send you a free audit that tells you where your leads are coming from."

**Lead with the thing they GET, not with what you'll do.** "We'll send you a free audit" is a deliverable. "I'll grade your site and send it back" is a description of your afternoon.

**Name people the first time.** "Nate paid $45,000 a year for a phone that stopped ringing at five" - never open on "He" and make the reader hold a pronoun until you tell them who it is.

**2. One contestable opinion per piece.** If nobody could disagree with a single line, nobody has any reason to remember it. Name what's broken and who benefits from it staying broken.

- Flat: "Conversion tracking is often not set up correctly."
- Alive: "Your agency has never once mentioned conversion tracking. That's not an oversight."

Note what the alive version does: it's SHORTER. An opinion doesn't need explaining - the accusation lands harder implied than spelled out. If a punchy line takes two clauses and a re-read, it isn't punchy, it's just long.

**3. Break the rhythm.** Four tidy paragraphs of even length is the tell. Put a three-word sentence after a long one. Interrupt yourself. Use a bracket. Answer your own question.

- Flat: "The results speak for themselves and clients are consistently satisfied."
- Alive: "He saved six figures a year. Nobody dials a phone any more."

**Not this:** "Same phone, same leads, nobody dialling." Three trailing fragments in a row is its own tic - it turns up in every piece of AI copy written this decade, and it reads as a copywriter performing rather than a person talking. Two sentences, one longer and one short, does the same job without the costume.

## Finish the sentence. Then say who did it.

Punchy is not the same as unfinished, and this is where the two get confused.

**Never narrate the technique.** "Here's the line I'll pick a fight over." "Let me be blunt." "Here's the uncomfortable truth." Nobody announces an opinion before having it - it's throat-clearing, and it tells the reader you think the opinion needs a run-up. Delete the announcement and start at the opinion.

**Finish every contrast you start.** If a sentence says what something ISN'T, it has to say what it IS. Leaving the second half implied looks like confidence and reads like a missing clause.

- Broken: "An agency that reports clicks instead of customers isn't confused."
- Fixed: "An agency that reports clicks instead of customers knows exactly what it's doing. Clicks always go up."

**Name who's doing it.** Passive voice hides the actor and drains the sentence.

- Broken: "When we build a lead machine, tracking gets wired in first."
- Fixed: "The first thing I wire up is the tracking."

**Vague nouns kill a line faster than a bad joke.** "The number that can embarrass everybody" is a riddle. Say the number, or say the thing.

The check: read the sentence cold, with nothing before it. If you have to reconstruct what it means, it isn't written yet.

## Write for someone who has never heard of you

Every surface that reaches a cold reader - a GBP post, a blog post from search, an ad - gets read by somebody with zero context. They have not seen your homepage. They do not know your product names, and they will not look them up.

**Any word you invented, or your industry invented, gets said in plain English.**

- Broken: "Start a Sprint now and it's live by early November." What's a Sprint?
- Fixed: "Start now and it's live by early November." Nothing was lost.

This applies to your own product names, your service tiers, and every bit of trade shorthand: speed to lead, growth engine, the foundation, lead machine, map pack, schema, attribution. Fine on your own site once the reader has context. Noise on the first thing they ever read from you.

**Product names are allowed AFTER the plain description, never instead of it.** "Our eight-week build - we call it the SEO Sprint" works. "Book a Sprint" does not.

The check: hand the sentence to someone who has never seen your website. Do they know what you are offering?

**Say why it's bad. Never imply it.** A line that leaves the reader to work out the accusation is a riddle, and riddles get skipped.

- Broken: "Your agency reports clicks because clicks always go up." Why is that bad? You have to solve it.
- Fixed: "The only thing that matters when you run ads is profit. Clicks are a vanity metric - they don't deliver the one result you're actually after."

The second one is longer and it lands, because nothing is left for the reader to assemble.

## Where the line is

Punch **up and out**, never down or in.

**The villain is never a person.** Punch at situations and objects - the retainer with no end date, the login that lives in someone else's inbox, the report full of clicks, the pipe that always picks the nice bathroom. Never at people, named or unnamed: "nine out of ten agencies are crooks" is an insult wearing the business's name, whatever word you soften it with. The test: could the owner read the line aloud to a room with their competitors in it and still look generous?

**Fair game:** the industry's habits, retainers with no end date, jargon, reports that count clicks instead of money, the absurdity of the trade, yourself on effort and taste.

**Over the line, every time:** the customer or reader · a named competitor (it's a legal problem as well as a bad look) · anyone's protected characteristics · politics · your own competence at the thing you sell · anything you'd have to explain to a client afterwards.

The test: **would you say it, out loud, to a prospect sitting across the table?** If yes it's punchy. If you'd soften it first, soften it now.

## The industries where humour is switched off

In these categories humour isn't risky, it's disqualifying. Your reader is in the worst week of their life:

**Funeral and cremation · personal injury · oncology and serious illness · bankruptcy and debt · addiction and recovery · divorce and family law · criminal defence · end-of-life veterinary · mental health crisis · abuse and trauma services.**

Dial goes to zero. Warmth, plain language and certainty replace wit, and the retention levers carry the page. Say the switch out loud so it reads as deliberate, not as a flat draft.

**Adjacent but fine:** dentists, vets, conveyancing and wills, accountants, clinics. Normal service, dial slightly down. When genuinely unsure, ask - the cost of asking is a sentence, the cost of a joke in a funeral home is the client.

## When not to joke, even at full dial

- **Inside a proof block.** A joke next to a price reads as hedging.
- **On the reader's crisis moment.** Describe the 2am flood vividly. Joke about the plumber's back, never about their panic.
- **In an FAQ or answer-first block.** See the precedence rule above.
- **In the CTA.** The last line before the ask is straight, always.

## Calibration

- **PG-13.** Mild profanity only if it is genuinely the owner's voice. Punch at problems, prices and the trade's absurdities - never at customers, named competitors, or protected groups. No politics on a plumbing blog.
- **Universal references.** Jokes a 30-year-old and a 60-year-old both get. No memes that date the page in six months, and no reference that needs decoding: "someone's childhood memories of Tuscany" is abstract and died on a real page; "scrolling past the writer's life story to find the actual ingredients" is the same joke in plain sight. Grade 10 applies to the jokes too.
- **Some jokes will miss.** Cut those on re-read. Never let fear of missing produce a boring page.

## The test, on every draft

Read it aloud. After each paragraph: **would a real person keep reading this at 11pm?** Is there one line per section someone might screenshot? A paragraph of pure dutiful information is not done - find the angle, the analogy, or the story.

Two checks that don't rely on taste:

1. **Count the contractions.** None means it was written by a machine impersonating a lawyer.
2. **Read the jokes on their own.** If a line needs the paragraph around it to be funny, it's a sentence, not a joke.

## Nobody grades their own jokes

A draft never comes with a note explaining why it's funny, and no line gets tagged with the device it used. Whoever wrote the joke is the worst judge of it - they always like it, and the explaining becomes a substitute for fixing. The draft goes out plain. You laugh or you don't.

# Want your own writing style instead?

Say so and it gets extracted from your published long-form writing - articles, posts, scripts, emails where you explained something to a customer.

Never from Slack messages or two-line replies. Those are a different job at a different pitch, and blending them is how content ends up sounding like nobody.

**Style comes from Part 1. Material comes from you.** Most people are not writers, so extracting a style faithfully means faithfully reproducing weak writing. If you have a clear kind of funny - dry, deadpan, dad-joke - it gets matched. If you do not, the house style leads rather than flattening to nothing.
