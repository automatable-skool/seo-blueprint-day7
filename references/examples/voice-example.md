## The voice in one line

A real plumbing crew that's seen everything, talking to a stressed homeowner like a smart, funny friend who happens to know exactly what's wrong and what it costs. Conversational and generous, never corporate.

## Where the dials sit

**Casual, not formal** - well past halfway.
"Put a bucket under it, pour yourself something."

**Playful, not serious** - a light beat per line, dead serious only on safety and cost.

**Bold, not reserved** - we say the quiet part, including where the money really goes.

**Personal, not corporate** - all the way personal. "We", "our crew", "the techs". Humans, not a brand.

**Punchy, not measured** - one-line paragraphs, fragments for emphasis.

**Educational, not salesy** - all the way educational. Teach first. The upfront honesty *is* the sales pitch.

## How we open

Hook with a relatable, funny, true observation - a joke that's also accurate - then open the gap the reader needs closed.

- "Nobody googles 'how much does a plumber cost' on a good day."
- "Let me guess. You're standing over a leak with a towel, expecting the answer to be 'a lot.'"

## How we close

A wry callback or button, then the call to action. Never a corporate sign-off.

- Call back the bucket, the drip, or the dinner the plumber left.
- Then the ask: "We're Ready to Serve You", leading to "Book An Appointment".

## Sentence rhythm

Short. Often one sentence per line. Fragments for punch. Long sentences only when unspooling a point.

- "It isn't. The hourly rate isn't a wage. It's the whole business folded into one number."
- "A $200 repair today, or a $4,000 water-damage bill next winter. One of these is cheaper."

## Emotional register

Calm, been-there reassurance with a dry grin. We find the comedy in the universal misery of home ownership - the holiday burst pipe, the $4 part that ruins a week - and we laugh *with* the homeowner, never at them. When safety or money is genuinely on the line, we drop the jokes and say it straight.

## What we never do

- No corporate jargon or hype - "seamless", "leverage", "game-changer".
- No punching down at the customer for not knowing plumbing.
- No profanity. Keep it PG-13 and clean.
- No personal, creator or other-business references. There is only Plumbera.
- No long unbroken paragraphs.

## Formatting tells that make it sound like us

- A spaced hyphen " - " in place of an em dash, for asides and reveals: "Now - the honest part."
- One-line paragraphs with line breaks between them.
- Numbered or bulleted lists for scannable takeaways.
- Concrete numbers and times, for proof and for comedy: "$26 part", "2am on a long weekend".

## Brand lines to weave in naturally

**Relatable problem framing**
"Nothing's worse than a basement that floods on a holiday... Yuck!"

**Action and reassurance**
"Not on our watch!" · "We're Ready to Serve You."

**Trust anchors**
Upfront pricing · No after-hours surcharge · 4.9 stars from 1,000+ reviews · Licensed and insured · 25-year warranty · Same-day service

## Humour

The rulebook for being funny in Plumbera's content, so posts get read instead of skimmed.
Worked example from the demo build, built June 2026.
Next: write the jokes, then check the draft against the guardrails at the bottom before it ships.

The goal: a reader should smile, or at least exhale through their nose, on nearly every line, then get a genuinely useful answer the second the joke lands. Built from [Writing Humor: The Art of Making Readers Laugh](https://www.hireawriter.us/creative/writing-humor-the-art-of-making-readers-laugh).

## The six rules

**1. Be yourself.**
For us, "yourself" is a real plumbing crew that has seen everything. We've crawled under the sink. We've pulled the thing out of the drain that you do not want to know about. That lived-in, been-there voice is the funniest and most trustworthy thing we own. Never write like a brochure. Write like the plumber who's seen your exact disaster forty times this year and isn't fazed.

**2. Keep it light-hearted.**
No politics, no mean-spirited digs, nothing that dunks on the customer for not knowing plumbing. Charged or controversial lines alienate part of the audience. The reader is already stressed and standing in water. Our humour is a release valve, never a put-down. We laugh **with** the homeowner at the universal absurdity of home ownership, never **at** them.

**3. Use universal references.**
Lean on things every homeowner knows - the 2am drip, the basement that floods on a holiday, the part that costs $4 but ruins your week, the dread of getting a quote. Skip inside-baseball plumbing jargon as a punchline. Nobody laughs at a joke they have to look up.

**4. Keep it PG-13.**
Our audience is stressed homeowners, including the family kind. Dry, clean, clever. No profanity, no crude stuff. The funniest line in the room is allowed to be the cleanest one. If a joke needs an edge, make the edge **honesty**, not vulgarity.

**5. Frequent, but small.**
The usual advice is to sprinkle humour on sparingly. Our dial is deliberately higher, because top-of-funnel blog content lives or dies on whether it's fun to read, so we aim for a light beat on nearly every line. We keep that safe by keeping the jokes **small and low-risk** - observational, dry, one-clause asides - and by making the useful information land clean right after. Frequent is not the same as loud. We're not doing a stand-up set, we're being the wittiest, most honest voice in a boring category.

**6. Don't fear failure.**
Writing jokes is a skill, not a gift, and even the best comedians have jokes that bomb. Write the risky line. If it's a groaner, cut it. A draft with ten jokes where three are great beats a safe draft with zero.

## The ten moves that actually get the laugh

Use the relatable, low-risk ones constantly. Use the bigger swings sparingly.

**Relatable observation** - our workhorse. Name the exact thing the reader is living.
"Nobody googles 'how much does a plumber cost' on a good day."

**Understatement** - undersell the disaster.
A flooded basement becomes "a minor water feature you didn't ask for."

**Comic exaggeration** - oversell the trivial.
"Three call-out fees in one week is how you personally fund a plumber's vacation."

**Misdirection** - set up an expectation, then swerve.
"You're not paying for the drip. You're paying for the dinner he left to come fix it."

**Rule of three** - two straight, one funny.
"A burst pipe, a dead water heater, and sewage making a comeback."

**Specificity** - concrete beats vague, every time.
"$26 part" beats "a cheap part". "2am on a long weekend" beats "after hours".

**Personification** - give the plumbing a personality.
"A drain that's been quietly plotting against you for a week."

**Self-deprecation, aimed at the trade and never the customer.**
"Plumbers have a reputation. We've earned about half of it."

**Comic contrast** - tiny cause, huge effect.
"A $4 washer and a $4,000 ceiling, connected by the same slow leak you've been ignoring."

**Callback** - replant an earlier joke later for a free laugh, such as bringing the bucket back at the end.

## Where the jokes go

- Open every piece with a relatable, funny, true observation. The hook is a joke that's also accurate.
- Put a light beat in the first sentence of most paragraphs, then deliver the real value clean.
- Let the genuinely important safety and cost information breathe. When the advice actually matters - water near electrical, get it in writing - say it straight. Earned seriousness hits harder after a run of jokes.
- Close on a callback or a wry button, never a corporate sign-off.

## Guardrails - do not cross

- **Never punch down at the customer.** Jokes are at the trade's expense, the situation's expense, or home ownership in general. Never the reader's intelligence.
- **PG-13 and clean.** No profanity, no crude imagery. This is a family-facing local business.
- **Stay light.** No politics, religion, or anything divisive.
- **Accuracy first.** A joke never distorts a real number, a safety warning or a guarantee. Funny and wrong is just wrong.
- **One groan maximum.** A single dad-joke-grade pun per piece is charming. Two is a war crime.

## Swipe file - the tone we're aiming at

- "Nobody googles 'how much does a plumber cost' on a good day."
- "Plumbing has a cruel rule: the prettier the room, the more the repair costs."
- "The universe has a sense of humour, so pipes almost exclusively burst on holidays."
- "Put a bucket under it, pour yourself something, and book a normal daytime visit. The drip will still be there in the morning. They always are."
- "A plumber who won't put a number on paper is telling you something - and it rhymes with 'run.'"
- "Water actively redecorating your home."
- "That suspiciously cheap quote isn't a bargain. It's a deposit on a second visit."

Lighter, situational brand staples: "Not on our watch!" · "Yuck!" · "We're ready to serve you."

## Vocabulary

## Signature phrases

- "Here's the thing" and "Now -", to concede a point before reframing it.
- "Here's the short version first."
- "No surprises" and "upfront, in writing."
- "The honest part" and "the part nobody tells you."
- "Put a bucket under it."
- "We've seen it" and "we've pulled worse out of a drain."
- "Book it for a normal daytime visit."

## Transitions we lean on

- "The problem?" - a one-word question to pivot.
- "And honestly,"
- "Here's the thing"
- "Now - to be fair,"
- Dash-led asides: " - not because X, but because Y"

## Words we never use

These are the AI tells and the hype words. Keep every one of them out.

delve · leverage · unlock · game-changer · supercharge · elevate · seamless · robust · look no further

Also banned as whole phrases: "in today's fast-paced world" · "navigate the landscape" · "it's important to note" · "furthermore" · "moreover"

Two more hard rules: never an em dash, always a spaced hyphen " - " instead. And no profanity, because this is a family-facing local business.

## Contractions and person

Heavy contractions - "we're", "don't", "it'd", "you're", "that's". First-person plural for the company - "we", "our crew". Direct second person for the reader - "you". Plain-spoken and warm, like a friend who happens to be a plumber.

## Spelling and conventions

**Canadian English**, because this is a Toronto business: odours, behaviour, neighbour, litre. Use "labour" for effort, though "labor" is fine inside an industry term when quoting a US source. Default to Canadian.

**Numerals for emphasis and stats**: "60 to 90 minutes", "18,000+ jobs", "$26 part".

**Starting sentences with "And", "But" and "So"** is fine and encouraged.

## Plumbing terms we use freely

drains · clogged drain · drain cleaning · emergency service · same-day service · upfront pricing · fully stocked truck · licensed and insured · call-out, trip or diagnostic fee · flat rate versus hourly

More of them: backflow · backwater valve · water heater, tank and tankless · sump pump · P-trap · shut-off valve · PEX · copper · repipe · sewer main · rough-in · journeyperson · master plumber · apprentice · WSIB

## Punctuation habits

- A spaced hyphen " - " is the workhorse for asides and reveals.
- One-line paragraphs, with line breaks for pacing.
- Exclamation marks used warmly but sparingly: "We're ready to serve you!"
- No social emoji in the blog body.

## Reading level

Plain-spoken, around a grade 6 to 8 level. Short sentences, concrete words, no filler. It should read like a smart, funny friend talking, not a brochure.
